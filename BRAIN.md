# Hive brain

Read this first. It is the shared memory for anyone working on Hive, human or agent: what we know, how sure we are, what is being tested, and exactly how to continue. When you learn something, update this file in the same commit as the evidence.

Last updated: Oct 30 2026, after Gate 49 and Gate 45 day 1.

## 1. The question

Can a swarm of AI coding agents that never message each other build real software by coordinating only through a shared git repository? And what limits how big and how correct such a swarm can get?

## 2. Current answer

On projects up to 1,340 tests and 12 agents: yes. Silent swarms finished every project they were given. They never gamed the tests, and collisions were rare and cheap.

Two things limit them today:

- **Cost is mostly onboarding.** Each worker spends about 110k tokens getting started, against about 3k to 5k per task after that.
- **Speed stops improving at around 6 to 12 workers.** On a 2-CPU machine the guard's full-suite runs per landing queue for the processor; on 24 cores the same 12-worker project finished in 3.5 minutes; limited to 4 CPUs on the same machine, 12.1 (Gates 10, 21). Caching main's test results in the guard saved about a fifth of the finish (0.84× and 0.72×, Gates 21, 22). Claiming the largest work first did not help (Gate 10).

The deeper limit is intent, and Gates 3 to 17 have now mapped it. On rules a model cannot guess, swarms pass every visible example but only 35 to 58% of hidden tests when nobody tells them the rules.

- **Builder questions help but hit a floor.** 30 questions add about +25 to +32 points (five rule sets). But builders only probe what they can imagine: about a fifth of errors survive any number of questions (Gate 12). The bottleneck is where questions point, not how many.
- **When the owner points, intent gets through.** One worked example per policy decision, chosen by the owner and given as tests (a *decision tour*), took builders to 89–96% hidden on three rule sets; with 17 questions on top, 95.2% and 98.2% (Gates 13–15, 17).
- **An owner with documents gets most of the way.** An extractor agent reads the handbook and picks inputs; the owner answers; handbook, answered examples and questions ship together: 93.8% on the same rules where the listing owner reached 98.2% (Gate 17). The gap sits entirely at decisions nobody wrote down (0.86 vs 1.00 of those errors fixed).
- **On a real company's rules (Gate 18):** 56 owner-confirmed examples took hidden correctness from 26.0% to 64.8%, and with 19 questions to 78.0% (Gate 7's best on the same rules: 49.6%). But the examples were agent-drafted QA cases the owner only confirmed, and they fixed no more than 19 builder questions (ratio 0.93). Conjecture: the examples' advantage needs the owner to *choose* the points.
- **The whole v1 design works as a system (Gate 24):** silent swarm, cached guard, owner's decision tour and 17 questions, at 6 workers: 95.0% hidden against 53.0% without the intent interface, with no breaking commit on main.
- **The founder's own examples (Gate 20):** on the real rules, 58 founder-written examples took hidden correctness from 23.6% to 72.4% (agent-drafted, Gate 18: +38.8 vs founder +48.8), fixed 1.17× the errors 19 questions did, and broke 5 calls against 34. With questions, 83.6%; with the company's own documents too, 84.8%. The documents covered only 2 of 10 functions, settled those completely and changed nothing elsewhere.
- **Hive v1 on a real codebase (Gate 25):** the tool built a real backlog feature in a real company's backend: 23/23 hidden acceptance tests, 384/384 existing tests, 10 minutes, $3.18, no code review. But the founder delegated 11 of 12 answers to an AI proxy holding his written decisions, which also shaped the hidden tests, so the result is optimistic.
- **With plain-language questions, the founder answered every one himself (Gate 26):** 4 of 4, median about 100 s, on a second real feature: 18/18 acceptance cases from the company's documents, 384/384 existing tests, $2.26. In Gate 25, with code-worded questions, he answered 1 of 12.
- **Live questions did not carry the founder's unwritten rules (Gate 27, kill fired):** a swarm that asked him (9 of 9 answered, median 61 s) and an identical swarm that could not ask both passed the same 19 of 24 of his cases, 6 of 10 head-only. His answers only confirmed the defaults the no-question swarm guessed anyway. The 5 misses were rules that depart from the obvious default, and nobody asked about them. Builders ask where they are uncertain, not where they are wrong (conjecture).
- **Unwritten rules matter only where they differ from the default (Gates 27, 28):** builders' defaults already matched the founder on 6 of 10 head-only cases in Gate 27 and 14 of 15 in Gate 28. Questions again tied with no questions (14/15 each), because one answer fixed a case and a stretched answer broke another. The assumption review could not be measured: there was no room, and the founder was away, so the review was by proxy.
- **Kept rulings did not separate the arms (Gate 29, kill fired), and memory halved the review list:** with and without the founder's 15 kept rulings, both swarms passed 5 of 5 cases built from them. The founder's 4 changes had all been Hive's own suggestions, which a builder reaches without memory. Memory cut the owner's business assumptions from 25 to 13. Three gates now agree: intent channels add value only where the owner differs from the model's default (model).
- **First measured gain from the owner on surprising rules (Gate 30), smaller than predicted:** on 6 cases where the founder differs from a cold model's default (blind-judged), his phone review raised the code from 2/6 to 3/6 (+17 points; predicted ≥ +30; kill < +10 not fired). Builders recorded assumptions touching all 6 rules, but only 3 ranked into the 8 he saw. On one, a menu of alternatives led him to a rule contradicting his own verdict from an hour earlier.
- **Scanning every decision fixes coverage; the answering step then failed (Gate 31, kill fired):** with all 29 decisions listed in one line, the founder marked a matching decision for 8 of his 9 surprising rules (Gate 30: 3 of 6 shown). Then he kept all 22 he had marked, in 2 to 12 s each, so nothing changed (3/9 both arms). Marked decisions now ask for his way first, and three quick keeps prompt a check.
- **A model of the founder cannot stand in for him (Gate 33):** given his business and his past verdicts, a model predicted 7 of his 20 surprising rules on held-out gates (35%; the cold default gets 0%) and 79% of ordinary ones. His surprising rules are mostly new in each area. Gate 32 was not run: only 5 surprising cases in selection and invitations (credits had 9), so how surprising an owner is depends on the area.
- **The review mechanism works end to end when the owner answers (Gate 34b, synthetic owner):** with 8 deliberately unusual rules, a model-owner drove Hive's real review code. Unusual rules went from 0/8 (no owner) to 7/8 (after review and one fix round); coverage 8/8 recorded, 8/8 marked, 11/11 marked changed; 384/384 kept. The gap in Gates 30-31 is the human answering step, not the machinery. A stand-in model of the founder gave only 1 surprising verdict of 12 (Gate 34, not run).
- **The owner's rules come out when he answers situations before the build, not when he audits decisions after it (Gates 30-35):** across 48 situations he answered every one (28 differing from the default) and confirmed 16 of 16 readings; reviewing builders' decisions he found half (30), kept all he found (31), and in Gate 35 marked none of 27 (kill fired; 1/7 both arms). Next: interview first, with the owner's answers becoming tests before the build (Gate 36).
- **An interview Hive writes from the description alone did not carry the owner's surprising rules (Gate 36, kill fired):** both arms passed 2 of 7 surprising hidden cases. Hive's 12 questions covered 4 of 9 surprising hidden situations (2 of the 7 scored), and on both covered ones the founder's interview answer went the opposite way from his hidden-set verdict on the same situation that day. Two limits for every intent channel: ask about the decisions the build will make, not what the description suggests (conjecture), and the owner's rule can depend on how the situation is framed (seen 2 of 2, this owner). The interview took 124 minutes, 102 of them lost to a tool bug (silently dropped taps), now fixed along with resuming after any stop.
- **With full coverage, the limit is the owner's own consistency (Gate 37, kill fired):** an interview written from the code's decisions covered 6 of 6 of his surprising situations, and the builders followed his answers. But his answers to two framings of the same decision agreed on 1 of 8 in the same session (5 different, 2 too vague) and 3 of 6 a day apart, so the score stayed at 0/4 in both arms, and arm I lost 2 ordinary cases. Established for this owner (14 pairs, blind-judged): a single answer to a concrete situation is not a stable rule. Conjecture: the question creates the rule, so Hive must get each decision confirmed once as a general rule, and resolve disagreeing answers with him.
- **A rule he confirmed does not hold a day later either (Gate 38, kill fired):** Hive's interview ended with the rule sheet; he confirmed 13 general rules in 7.9 minutes. A day later his verdicts matched those rules on 2 of the 11 decisions they covered (blind judge), the same as his raw answers (2 of 11). The builders followed his rules exactly: +3 surprising cases where a rule held (3/6 vs 0/6), −3 ordinary ones where he changed his mind (2/6 vs 5/6), a tie overall (5/12). Established for this owner, across Gates 36–38: neither an answer nor a confirmed rule predicts his next-day decision. Conjecture: his decision is made when he faces a concrete outcome, so the hand-over (the code sheet: what was built, marked by him) is the main intent channel, and the interview only seeds it.
- **With a perfect owner, the machine leaks 24 points, and none of it is the builders (Gate 39, synthetic owner):** given the owner's complete rule sheet in prose, 3 builders passed 250 of 250 hidden tests and broke nothing. Hive's interview, answered by a synthetic owner who holds the exact rules, reached 76.0% (the decision tour 75.6%, no owner 38.4%). Of 25 decisions, 8 were never asked, all about unusual input (unreadable dates, letter case, separators, leftover fractions), which the planner skips as "technical". 4 asked ones were lost before the sheet: 1 to a Hive bug ("mostly right, but…" replaced the whole rule, now fixed), 2 to a harness defect, 1 stated in part. Model: what fixes errors is the share of decisions that reach the builders stated whole.
- **Asking about unusual input did not raise coverage; the complete sheet replicated (Gate 40):** a planner told to list unusual input planned 40 decisions but asked about 16 of 25 (the shipped one 19) and scored 64.4% vs 63.2%; it does not ship. The complete rule sheet passed 250 of 250 again (500 of 500 over two rule sets, 0 broken); the tour 84.0%. Across three interviews the sheet kept 0.69 to 0.76 of the decisions asked, lost in Hive's merge into one rule per function and in questions skipped as "settled". Model: the interview delivers about half the decisions whole (coverage about 0.7, then about 0.72 kept).
- **The merge was not the leak; questions about cases are (Gate 41, kill fired):** from one interview, the sheet alone scored 72.8% and the sheet plus every ruling kept whole 66.0% (−6.8, CI −16.0 to +0.8, with 16 vs 6 calls broken: builders stretched the rulings). Both stated the same decisions right. The loss was upstream: the questions covered 11 of 25 decisions, and his answers stated only 7 of those 11 whole (asked about one case, he answered for that case). The complete sheet passed 250 of 250 a third time. Conjecture: a sheet written from the built code is complete by construction, so the owner only has to mark what is wrong (Gate 42).
- **Breakthrough with a synthetic owner: verify, don't elicit (Gate 42).** A describer wrote the first build's code as 68 plain rules; the owner marked the 34 that were not his policy, said each one right, and added what was missing. Fresh builders given that sheet passed 249 of 250 hidden tests (99.6%), against 68.4% from Hive's interview (+31.2, CI +15.2 to +49.6) and 100% from his own complete sheet. The code sheet covers what the code does (20 of 25 decisions; it cannot show a cap the code lacks), and the review repaired 16 of 18 wrong rules and added all 5 missing. Shown a whole rule, the owner returns a whole rule. Open: will a real owner read and correct a 60-rule sheet? Now Hive's hand-over.
- **Talking did not help (Gate 43, asked for by the user):** with a shared message board, 6-worker teams finished the scale project in 3.6 and 3.5 minutes against 3.5 and 3.4 silent, at the same cost ($4.20 vs $4.15) and correctness (all 1,340 visible; 99.8% vs 99.7% hidden). They posted 4–5 useful messages per team (shared machinery, a merge hotspot, a changed interface), which claims and commits already carry. Coordination is not the bottleneck at this scale; Hive stays silent.
- **Rules and talking together (Gate 44, asked for by the user):** a 2 × 2 on fresh rules. The corrected code sheet took builders from 45.2% to 100% silent and 49.2% to 100% talking; talking added +4.0 without rules (CI 0 to +11.6) and 0.0 with them. The code sheet replicated (99.6% in Gate 42, 100% here). Builders on the board shared guesses and split work; a consistent guess is still a guess. Hive stays silent.
- **A lead with a shared brain lost to a flat swarm (Gate 46, asked for by the user):** on a deep project (from a 221-line skeleton to 1,340 tests), a lead that mapped dependency levels and interfaces in 35 s, over 6 builders, reached 95% in 6.2 and 6.7 minutes; 7 flat builders in 4.4 and 6.3 (lead 1.20× slower, 1.09× costlier, same 99.8% hidden). The plan serialized work onto the foundation, the critical path; flat builders hacked pieces in parallel. Coupling stayed low even from a skeleton. Hive stays flat.
- **Claude and Codex in one swarm (Gate 47, asked for by the user):** two mixed teams (2 Claude + 2 Codex builders) built the deep project to 1,340/1,340 and 100% hidden in 9.2 and 8.1 minutes to 95%, against 7.5 (4 Claude) and 8.3 (4 Codex), with no break of main in any team; Codex made 52% and 73% of the mixed teams' landings. A Codex lead planned in 85 s and its team matched the Claude-led one (8.0 vs 8.3 min). The protocol is agent-neutral; mixing halves the Claude bill. Hive takes `--agent claude,codex`.
- **Trained on his style, the fast reader takes half the reading (Gate 49):** Laya trained on 444 owner-style messages read 77.5% of the 120 held-out ones right (Hindi or mixed 73.8%; 73.8% on the 80 sharing no phrase with training) at 0.55 s. Answering only at confidence 0.5 or more, it took 56% of messages, and Laya-then-Claude read 94.2% against Claude's 92.5%. Ship fired: Jarvis uses it when installed, only for meanings needing no details, never on builder commands.
- **The founder's paged code-sheet review (Gate 45, day 1):** he finished all 9 pages and marked none of 46 rules (P2 failed; as in Gate 35). Live faults it exposed, fixed: a question was filed as a correction and the page waited; 46 rules came as 40 pages; rules used the code's own words. Rules 19-46 were reworded in plain words before he reached them (disclosed). Day 2 decides whether the sheet held his rules.
- **A fast decision model does not read a founder's texts (Gate 48, asked for by the user):** Laya, the open copy of Jev, read 42.5–45.0% of 120 owner-style messages right against Claude's 92.5%, and 31–41% of Hindi or mixed ones; tuned on a separate practice set it still read 45.0%. Once held to 2 threads it answered in 0.68 s against 4.2 s, but even its most confident answers were wrong 1 time in 5, so no hand-off threshold exists. Claude stays Jarvis's reader; training Laya on the owner's style is the open question.
- **The swarm still beats one agent, as a speed-for-money trade (Gate 50):** on the deep project with today's model, one builder finished in 12.8 minutes for $2.94; seven in 4.2 minutes (3.0×) for $5.53 (1.9×); twelve were slower than seven at 3.4× the cost. Scalability fit: serial share 0.25, crosstalk 0.013 (equal to the law's κd), peak near 7.6. The law's 'one core per builder' was wrong for a fast suite (2 builders, 1.16×, where 7 gave 3.0× on the same 2 cores); fixed.
- **Team notes did not help 12 builders (Gate 51):** silent 5.2 vs notes 8.0 minutes to 95% (ABBA; runs 3.9 to 10.8), cost 1.04×; the notes were real interface contracts. Load on 2 cores averaged 1.1–3.2 and the slowest team had the lowest, so 12 builders vary by their own paths, not the CPU. The law's size trigger for notes is off; collisions alone turn them on. Five gates agree: talking does not speed the swarm.
- **The product flies end to end (Gate 52, flight test):** `hive start` as shipped, a synthetic owner answering from a private refund policy: goal to branch in 5.3 minutes, 22 of 22 hidden acceptance tests, $0.56 of builders, 20 owner prompts. Finding: two reviews where one does (20 assumptions one by one, then the code sheet); the code sheet now replaces the assumption review. The law sized the team before knowing the job (8, then 4, for one function).
- **The second flight (Gate 53):** after the fixes, the law put 1 builder on the one-function job ($0.24 against $0.56), there was no one-by-one review, 22/22 held, and the code sheet caught two real bugs the hidden tests missed (shipping on a damaged single item; 'not damaged' read as damaged), fixed by the owner's marks in one round.
- **A real swarm flies through the product (Gate 54):** three parts, three builders, a live builder question answered, 26/27 hidden as built and 27/27 after the code-sheet hand-over (it caught negative days), 7.4 minutes, $0.50 of builders. Its first launch found a fault: a closed input re-showed a builder's question 173 times while the builder waited; fixed. Across three flights the code sheet found and fixed the build's faults in 2 of 3 and made nothing worse.
- **The owner's attention is the limit of the code sheet (Gate 55):** one 83-rule sheet reviewed three ways. A careful owner repaired 19 of 19 wrong decisions (100% hidden); a skimming owner 5 of 19 with the list (65.2%) and 6 of 19 in Jarvis's conversational review (66.4%); no owner 36.0%. The conversation drew more replies (27 vs 13) but a skimmer accepts a summary that sounds right. Next lever: show fewer, sharper rules (only the business choices the code made).
- **Open:** an assumption review (builders list the defaults they applied, the owner marks the wrong ones); all 5 Gate 27 misses trace to defaults stated in the builders' own commit messages (post hoc). Also larger features with persistence and endpoints, and more founders and companies.

## 3. The law and how sure we are of each part

```
X_useful = min( N(1−f) / (1 + κ·d·(N−1)),   (1−f) / t_g,   I / b )
```

N agents; κ share of concurrent pairs that edit the same lines; d cost of one collision, in task lengths; f share of landings that carry a bug; t_g full-suite time of a central gate; I bits of intent entering per hour; b bits needed per unit of correct work.

Status tags: **established** means outside theory or our data on real agents; **model** means our formula, fitted on some cases and checked on held-out ones; **conjecture** means not yet tested.

## 7. Design as of now (Hive v2 plus protocol v2)

- Workers never talk. Long-lived workers take task after task, because onboarding costs about 20 tasks' worth of tokens.
- Claims name test categories (`gates/gate3/tools/claim.py`); overlapping claims are refused and races resolved by commit order.
- Landing is guarded (`gates/gate3/tools/land.py`): no new duplicate definitions, and no test that passes on main may fail after the change.
- Two suites: visible tests guide the workers, hidden tests judge. Hidden suites are never committed.
- No founder phase: the swarm shapes its own extension points within minutes.
- **Next changes:**
  - Guard caches main's suite results (Gates 21, 22: same safety, 0.84× and 0.72× the finish on a CPU-bound machine). Key the cache by code content, not commit: half of main's commits are lock commits that change no code (Gate 22). Give swarms about a core per worker.
  - Modules register themselves, which removes most conflicts.
  - `land.py --continue` squashes the fix into the blocked commit, so every commit on main passes.
- **Intent interface** (Gates 3, 4):
  - Write every answer into a shared test at once.
  - Label each answer as a ruling on that input only. Builders stretched rulings into rules, within and across functions (Gate 4: about 45 of 139 failing calls).
  - Planning questions by coverage (Gate 4) and asking about defaults first (Gate 5) did not beat free asking. Don't rely on either.
  - Spread a fixed budget across functions, about 2 per function before any gets a third. Blocks of 3 lost head to head (Gate 11: 1.25×), and within the same functions the 3rd question added nothing. The value of the 1st and 3rd questions varies with the rule set.
  - **Step one: the owner gives a decision tour**, one worked example per policy decision, as tests (Gates 13, 14: 57.8% → 89.4% and 42.4% → 95.6% hidden; 67.2% for the same number of builder-chosen answers). Do not add contrast examples (Gate 14: they moved boundaries). **Step two: builders ask questions around it** (95.2% with both). Builder questions alone hit a floor at decisions builders cannot imagine (Gate 12).
  - **When the owner has documents rather than a list:** ship the handbook, examples an extractor picked and the owner answered, and builder questions together; each layer added (Gate 16: 60.8% → 71.0% → 83.2%). Prefer the owner's own decision list when it can be had (about 10 points better). Treat a document's silence as unknown, not as "standard": a handbook that waved at "the standard" misled builders on an unwritten rule (Gate 16).
  - **Questions to the owner are plain language** (Gate 25): one real-life situation, amounts in rupees, 2 or 3 lettered choices. All 12 questions workers asked in Gate 25 were in code terms, and the founder delegated after the first; `hive ask` now refuses code words, symbols and status codes.
  - A question that confirms the builders' guess buys nothing; confirmation does not mean the function is right (Gate 8, conjecture).
  - **Interview first, but from the decisions the build will make** (Gate 36): questions written from the description alone covered 44% of where the owner differs. Write them from the interface or the builders' plan (conjecture). Save every answer as it comes, answer stale taps, read "you decide" as no rule, and put any answer that contradicts an earlier ruling back to the owner (all in the tool since Gate 36).
  - **Builds pick up again:** a builder stopped by a usage limit or an expired login waits, tells the owner, and resumes in its own clone (Gate 36; Gate 35's first build was written off).
  - **Rank the owner's review by where he differs, not by impact** (Gate 30): builders recorded all 6 surprising rules, but impact ranking showed only 3. **Ask open first on high-stakes decisions:** a menu of alternatives led the founder away from his own free-text rule (Gate 30). Check new answers against earlier rulings.
  - **A stretched answer can cost as much as the answer gains** (Gate 28): a founder's ruling on a wording-only change was stretched into "a different item never matters", which broke a case the swarm would otherwise have passed. Keep labelling each answer as a ruling on that input only, and consider having the owner confirm the rule a builder derives from an answer before it lands.
  - **Assumption lists need ranking** (Gate 28): 27 items, mostly error handling an owner cannot judge, and the one that mattered appeared only beside a related item. Show the owner the few business defaults that change outcomes, not every guard clause.
  - **Questions miss the rules that matter most** (Gate 27): builders ask where they are uncertain, and never about a default they are confident in. A real founder's 9 answers changed nothing a no-question swarm did not already get right; all 5 misses were confident defaults that differed from his rule. Next step: an assumption review, where builders list the defaults they applied in plain words for the owner to correct (conjecture, to be tested in Gate 28).
  - Put the allowed input values (statuses, types, categories) in the spec. Their absence caused the largest losses with a real company's rules (Gate 7).
  - Read the company's own documents first. They overturned two of the owner's rules before any code was written (Gate 7).
  - Vary one thing per question. A question that mixed a small group with an invalid value taught the wrong rule (Gate 5, `nps_score`).
  - **The goal is a complete rule sheet** (Gate 39): builders given every decision in plain words passed 250 of 250 and broke nothing; one example per decision fixed 0.85 and broke 20. Aim every intent channel at getting each decision stated whole.
  - **A correction amends the rule; it never replaces it** (Gate 39: "mostly right, but you left out a minimum" erased the rate and cap he had just confirmed). An answer in his own words counts as a choice only if it adds nothing the choice lacks. The terminal restates a rule before asking for its correction.
  - **Unusual input is a business decision** (Gate 39): all 8 decisions the interview never asked were what the product shows for an unreadable date, a lowercase code, separators or leftover fractions. But telling the planner so did not raise coverage (Gate 40), so `inputs=True` stays off.
  - **Hand over general rules, not case rulings** (Gate 41): adding his answers word for word beside the sheet cost 6.8 points and broke more (16 vs 6). The sheet's merge lost nothing measurable; the loss is that a question about a case returns a rule about that case.
  - **Builders stay silent; team notes switch on only when they collide** (Gate 51: size alone no longer turns them on; at 12 builders they were not faster) (Gates 43–44: a message board added nothing at 3–6 builders). A note is one line in a landing's commit, carried by git, not a chat. It turns on for 10+ builders or at 0.5 collisions per landing (conjecture, the measured 12-worker rate was 0.48); every rebase conflict shows the recent notes. Untested where it could matter: a large, tightly coupled codebase.
  - **Hand over a sheet written from the built code** (Gate 42, synthetic owner: 99.6% vs 68.4% from the interview): every behaviour as a whole rule; the owner marks what is wrong, says it right, adds what is missing; the changes go to a fix round. In the tool (`hive/codesheet.py`, `start.handover`). Keep the interview before the build for the decisions that change what gets built.

## 8. Open questions, most important first

000. **Will a real owner read and correct a code sheet?** With a synthetic owner, the corrected code sheet gave 99.6% (Gate 42). A real build's sheet runs to 50–80 rules; Gates 30–35 found real owners audit long lists of builder decisions poorly, though in Gate 38 the founder confirmed a 13-rule sheet of his own rules. Needs the founder: one real feature, the code sheet on his phone, his marks against his own later verdicts.
000a2. **Answered (Gate 42, synthetic):** a sheet written from the built code, corrected by the owner, nearly matches his complete sheet.
000a. **Answered (Gate 41):** keeping his rulings whole beside the sheet does not help (−6.8; more stretching).
000b. **Answered (Gate 40):** telling the planner that unusual input is an owner decision did not raise coverage (0.64 vs 0.76).

00. **Answered, no (Gate 38):** confirming a decision as a general rule does not make it stable. His confirmed rules matched his next-day verdicts on 2 of 11, the same as raw answers. Open in its place: is a verdict given while looking at what was built (the code sheet) stable? If even that drifts, the referee itself is unstable, and Hive should treat his latest word as the rule and make changing it cheap.
00b. **Answered (Gate 37):** where to ask. Questions written from the code's decisions cover his surprising rules (6 of 6). Questions written from the description do not (4 of 9 in Gate 36, 3 of 9 decisions-first).
0. **Cases where the owner is surprising.** Intent channels can only add value where the owner's rule differs from builders' defaults (Gates 27, 28: 4 of 10, then 1 of 15). Ask the owner first what people usually get wrong about the business, and build cases there. Then rerun the assumption review with a ranked, business-only list and the founder reviewing blind.
0b. **The assumption review (Gate 28, unmeasured).** Builders list the defaults they applied in plain words, and the owner marks the wrong ones. Does this recover the head-only rules that live questions miss (Gate 27: 6/10 with or without questions)?
1. **The intent curve.** How does hidden correctness grow with the number of answered values: 0, 10, 30, 100, 300 questions, repeated? This would turn limit 7 from evidence into a measured law.
2. **Real owners.** Every owner so far was an agent that wrote its own rules. Does the documented-owner interface hold with a real company's documents and a person answering? Does a cheap elicitation step ("here are the decisions we found; which are missing?") recover the unwritten ones (Gate 17's remaining gap)?
3. **The guard's floor.** CPU per guard check sets the finish on small machines (Gate 21: 3.5 → 12.1 minutes from 24 to 4 CPUs). Caching saved 16%; the model puts the floor near 5.9 minutes on that load. Does a near-100% cache plus checking only touched categories reach it without letting regressions through?
4. **The largest swarm this lab allows**: 20 workers with largest-first claiming.
5. **Turning intent into tests automatically**: the referee as the product.
6. **Breakers at scale**: do they shorten the 31-hour unseen-bug lifetime?

## Related systems

- **Literature check, Oct 28 2026 (`docs/related_work.md`):** the building blocks are not new: parallel agents through git (Anthropic's C compiler, Cursor's FastRender), clarifying questions (ClarifyGPT, AMBIG-SWE), user-confirmed tests (TiCoder), and above all **Specification by Example**, which the decision tour is. Possibly new (not found in a first-pass search): a randomized head-to-head of owner-chosen examples against builder questions, a measured question floor, and owner-chosen vs owner-confirmed. Systematic review needed before any public novelty claim.

- **T3 Code** (Ping.gg, open source; v0.1.0 on Oct 15 2026): a control surface for coding agents (Codex, Claude Code, Cursor and others) across desktop, web and phone. Parallel threads run in separate git worktrees. A person reviews diffs and approves, and `delegate_task` lets a parent agent start child agents.
  - **Contrast with Hive.** T3 Code helps one person *supervise* several agents, with the person as referee. Hive removes the supervisor: agents coordinate through claims and guarded landings, and tests are the referee.
  - **Model, not measured on T3 Code.** A person reviewing every landing is a serial gate (limit 2). Our 12-worker swarm landed about 4 changes a minute, so at 2 minutes per review it would need about 8 reviewers. Hive moves the person to intent (limit 7) instead.
  - **They complement each other.** T3 Code could serve as a Hive dashboard; Hive cannot work where there is no referee, and T3 Code can.

## 10. What this lab allows

- At most 20 agents at once per session. A launch beyond that silently starts only the first 20.
- A usage limit stops all agents mid-run. It hit after roughly 4M subagent tokens in one window. Plan runs to fit, and record partial runs as aborted.
- 2 CPUs. Every guarded landing runs the suite twice, so timings include CPU contention. Compare swarms only when they ran under the same load.
- The workspace container can restart: background servers die and must be restarted. Bare repos and files survive.
- Pushing to GitHub needs the claude.ai account linked to GitHub. Otherwise the work arrives as a git bundle that the user pushes.

## 11. How to continue

Open a Claude Code session on this repo (laptop, VM or phone) and say "Continue Hive: run the next ready experiment in lab/QUEUE.md".
- `lab/QUEUE.md` holds the handoff note and the ready experiments.
- `lab/README.md` explains the scripts.
- One command runs a whole intent gate: `python3 lab/run_gate.py lab/experiments/<config>.json`.

Rules of the lab:

- Register predictions before launching.
- Change one thing per experiment.
- Every worker logs CLAIM, LAND, CONFLICT and BLOCKED events to `HIVE_LOG`.
- Hidden suites and private intent stay out of the repo until their test has run, and real companies never appear.
- Report failures as plainly as successes.
- Give 95% intervals clustered by function, say when a comparison is observational, and remember that one run per team is not a replication.
